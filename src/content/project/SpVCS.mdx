## The Problem
Gait analysis is a clinical tool for diagnosing and monitoring gait disorders (disorders in walking pattern). Traditional gait assessment systems can provide highly accurate measurements, but they often rely on expensive laboratory-based equipment and controlled environments that do not reflect real life walking variabilities. Inertial sensors and smartphone video applications, on the other hand, offer portable alternatives but are currently limited by integration drift, lack of terrain information, the requirement of physical markers, and limited application in the natural environment.

This project explored whether a wearable system using a smartphone video could be used to assess gait during movement in a more natural environment or terrain while maintaining a consistent relationship between the camera and the participant. The main objective was to develop and validate a wearable, markerless smartphone video capture system capable of accurately capturing sagittal plane lower limb kinematics outside labs and clincs.

To address the identified problem, I defined the following user need:

<div class="user-need">
  <span class="user-need-label">User Need</span>
  <p>
    Enable individuals with lower limb mobility challenges to obtain gait data
    representative of their everyday movement in their natural environment in
    order to support remote gait assessment.
  </p>
</div>

## System Design
The final SpVCS design used a smartphone mounted to a custom 3D-printed two-rod assembly. The assembly attached to a waist belt through a rigid belt-mount interface and a 1/4" screw, allowing the system to be donned and removed as a wearable unit.

The system was designed around four core functions:
- Securely attach the device to the user without restricting movement
- Maintain a repeatable smartphone position and sagittal-plane view
- Capture video containing the required lower-limb gait events
- Process the video to extract gait events and temporal parameters

Objectives define what the system is intended to achieve, while metrics and criteria specify how those objectives are measured and what constitutes successful performance. The SpVCS objectives were divided into two areas: **device functionality**, which focused on capturing accurate and usable sagittal plane video for gait analysis, and **device operability and construction**, which focused on the system's form factor and usability.

### Mounting configuration selection
To satisfy the requirements for smartphone stability, user comfort, camera positioning, and unrestricted walking, I considered several mounting configurations for IMU sensors and 2D video recordings documented in existing literature. I then adapted these for the SpVCS and used a morphological chart to compare possible solutions across the major objectives of the system.

From the morphological chart, I determined that a **waist-mounted system** mostly satisfies the design requirements of the wearable system defined in Table 1. Compared to the chest, head and thigh, the waist provides a more stable reference frame relative to the body’s centre. This aids in reducing motion artefacts. Furthermore, more reliable sagittal plane capture of the two lower limbs is most possible if the wearable system is waist-mounted compared to head or chest-mounted.

### Preliminary risk analysis
To identify potential failure modes associated with the wearable SpVCS, I conducted a preliminary failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA). The goal of this analysis was to evaluate the impact of the identified risks on the validity and reliability of gait measurements and to inform design choices for the feasibility study and then the final wearable system. Since this system is a non-interventional measurement tool, severity ratings (see [Table 3](#table-severity)) were defined in terms of the accuracy and validity of extracted gait parameters.

From the FMEA, it was determined that the reliability and accuracy of gait parameters extracted from the wearable SpVCS depended heavily on the functional requirements of the system, like fixed mount geometry and non-occlusion of the feet.

### Feasibility study
Before starting on any physical design work, I conducted a feasibility study to determine whether a moving smartphone camera could produce gait-event measurements comparable with a fixed-camera reference.

A participant was recorded at a self-selected walking pace using a handheld smartphone approximately 0.21 m from the body. A second smartphone was fixed 0.38 m above the walking surface and used as the reference. Three trials were collected for each camera view, with approximately 38 gait cycles per trial.

## Video Processing and Gait Parameters Extraction
The obtained video was processed initially using Kinovea. However, since automatic tracking of the heel and toe was largely accurate, I opted to manually identify gait events using frame by frame inspection.
The extracted events were:
- Heel strike (HS)
- Toe off (TO)

These events were then used to calculate temporal parameters: stride, stance, and swing time using the formulas in Table 5.

### Conceptualization and Preliminary Design
The feasibility study informed the mounting location and camera requirements for the physical design. A parametric 3D human anthropometric model was then used in Fusion 360 to map the proposed geometry onto the body.
The mounting point was placed at the centre of the lower back. From this origin, rod 1 was projected 95 mm posteriorly to push the subsequent linked system outside the user’s immediate movement envelope. From the distal end of rod 1, rod 2 was projected inferiorly and posteriorly (away from the body) at a 45˚angle relative to the horizontal axis of rod 1. Finally, rod 3 was projected inferiorly and anteriorly (angling back toward the user) at a 45˚ angle relative to the vertical axis of rod 2.

A 123° horizontal field of view was used to model the smartphone camera. The target focal distance was established at 500 mm from the user’s foot, with a required horizontal coverage of at least 1000 mm to successfully capture two complete steps (from initial heel strike to terminal toe off). The length of rod 3 was iteratively adjusted within Fusion360 until the FOV cone completely covered the target horizontal tracking zone. This finalized rod 2 at a length of 300mm and rod 3 at 500mm, successfully positioning the camera lens at an optimal height of 530mm from the ground.

### Rod dimensioning
Rod dimensions were analytically determined to withstand the mechanical loads from the smartphone while maintaining elastic behaviour.

- **Design load:** 5.65 N effective tip load used for the worst-case bending case.
- **Material:** PETG with a reported yield strength of 50 MPa and a 10× dynamic safety factor, giving an allowable stress of 5 MPa.
- **Cross-section:** A rectangular section was selected to increase bending stiffness and provide flat mating surfaces.
- **Geometry:** A 20 mm rod width was selected to limit lateral deflection; the required thickness was calculated analytically and increased to provide additional geometric margin.
- **Interfaces:** The CAD design incorporated a 1/4" attachment interface, 50% thickness lap joints, and M5 bolt holes.
- **Stress reduction:** 5 mm fillets were added at reduced-thickness transitions and 3 mm fillets along outer edges.
- **Verification:** Joint fit and rod assembly were checked in CAD before static stress simulation.

### Static stress simulation
The initial and final designs were evaluated in Fusion 360 using a static stress simulation to assess their responses under the expected loading conditions. The initial four-rod design produced a minimum safety factor of 2.362. The final two-rod design increased the minimum safety factor to 3.272 and reduced the maximum tip displacement from 118.779 mm to 50.937 mm, a reduction of approximately 57%. The final design therefore provided greater static structural margin while reducing the deformation that contributed to camera movement. The simulation was a static load assessment and did not evaluate cyclic fatigue.

### Validation results
Bland Altman analysis was used to quantify the mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA) for each gait event and speed combination. Derived temporal gait parameters (stance time, swing time, and stride time) were subsequently compared between the two systems using grouped bar graphs with error bars representing the standard deviation across trials. All parameters were assessed separately for the right and left limbs. 

### Comparison with GaitRite®
Across both walking speeds, heel strike showed smaller bias and narrower limits of agreement than toe off. Increasing walking speed also increased both systematic bias and variability, with the largest disagreement observed for toe off at self-selected speed.

## Practical Implications of Findings
The findings from this project suggest that the wearable SpVCS has potential as a portable, lower-cost approach for gait analysis outside the laboratory. The strongest agreement with GaitRite was observed during super-slow walking, which is relevant to the target population of individuals with mobility impairments.
Compared with laboratory based gold standard systems, the SpVCS can be used without dedicated equipment or clinical space, supporting potential deployment in home and community settings. The results suggest that the system can provide useful approximations of stride time and, at super-slow walking speeds, stance and swing time.

The IMU comparison further demonstrated that stride time measurements remained consistent across different reference systems and walking conditions, supporting the potential of the wearable SpVCS for portable gait analysis in less controlled environments.

## System Limitations & Future Work
### Limitations
This project has limitations in both the experimental methodology and hardware performance.

**Experimental scope:** Validation was conducted with a single participant, limiting the ability to characterize SpVCS performance across a broader range of gait speeds and walking patterns. Gait events were also identified manually, frame by frame, by a single researcher. This introduces observer subjectivity, and inter-rater and intra-rater reliability were not assessed.

**Reference uncertainty:** Agreement was evaluated against GaitRite and ankle-worn IMUs, but neither should be treated as an error-free representation of true gait event timing. GaitRite has its own measurement uncertainty, while IMU measurements depend on sensor placement and algorithmic detection. The observed agreement therefore represents inter-system agreement rather than absolute accuracy. A consistent positive bias across conditions also suggests that synchronization and event extraction could be further calibrated.

**Hardware performance:** Vibrational noise increased at faster walking speeds, highlighting a structural limitation of the current design. Turning trials also introduced far-foot occlusion and were limited by a smaller number of observations. Finally, the study focused on temporal gait parameters and did not evaluate spatial measurements, which would require camera calibration to a known reference frame.

### Future Work
Future development of the SpVCS will focus on improving mechanical stability, strengthening validation, and automating gait analysis.

**System optimization:** The rod structure and geometry could be redesigned to reduce vibration at higher walking speeds. This could include evaluating higher stiffness materials and incorporating stabilization sensors without making the overall system bulky.

**Expanded validation:** Future studies would include a larger participant cohort, a wider range of gait speeds and walking conditions, and more complex movement in natural environments. Comparison with additional established video-based gait assessment systems would also help characterize agreement and reliability.

**Automated analysis:** A computer vision pipeline could replace manual gait-event identification, reducing observer subjectivity and improving analysis efficiency.

**Natural gait assessment:** Future studies should also investigate whether wearing the SpVCS alters the participant's natural walking pattern, particularly as the system is intended for use outside controlled laboratory environments.

## Reflection & Learnings
Working on this project taught me that engineering design is not about finding a single ideal solution. More often, it is about making tradeoffs between performance, usability, manufacturability, time, and project constraints. One of the biggest lessons came from the iterations on the rod design. Initially, I had included a requirement to have the full rod system to be an adjustable and foldable device. This was to improve portability and accommodate different users. As I continued to work on the design, I realized that these features added mechanical complexity and extended the prototyping timeline. Ultimately, I had to prioritize the requirements that were most critical to the system's core function, (that is, stable camera positioning, adequate field of view, structural performance, and ease of use) and simplify the design to meet the project timeline. Working through these compromises made the design process feel much less linear than I initially expected and taught me to treat iteration as part of the engineering process rather than evidence that an initial design was unsuccessful.

Going through the iterative design process reinforced several principles that are important in medical device development: starting from user needs, translating them into measurable requirements, evaluating risks early, iterating through prototypes, and verifying that the final design meets its intended performance. It also showed me the value of revisiting requirements as a design evolves rather than treating them as fixed once the project begins.

Another lesson I learned while working on this project was the importance of designing the system and its validation strategy together. Early design decisions such as camera position, mounting geometry, and field of view directly affected what could later be measured reliably. Iterating on the physical design therefore required considering not only whether the device could be built, but also whether its output could be meaningfully evaluated against a reference system. This reinforced the importance of traceability between requirements, design decisions, testing, and validation.

With more time, I would revisit the portability and adjustability requirements and investigate a more compact, foldable architecture without compromising structural stability or camera alignment. I would also expand the validation study to more participants and walking conditions and modify exisiting libraries like the YOLOv8 to automate gait event detection and reduce observer subjectivity.

In conclusion, I learned that good engineering is not only about designing what is technically possible but it is about understanding what matters most to the end users, making tradeoffs that enable the core functions of the system to be realized, and developing a solution that is appropriate for the user, the application, and the constraints of the project. Moving forward, this is the lens through which I intend to approach all my future design projects.
